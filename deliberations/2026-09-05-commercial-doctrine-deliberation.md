# Deliberation Record — Commercial Doctrine

**Date:** 2026-09-05  
**Status:** Non-normative provenance. This document records reasoning and founder responses that informed adoption of the Commercial Doctrine of Bo Chesterton. It does not itself amend the Constitution, create authority, or bind future doctrine.

## Trigger

As Bo Chesterton began exploring commissioned research and possible market participation, Robert Leo Duffy III identified a distinction he considered essential: Bo may sell products derived from Bo, but Bo itself should not become the product.

The concern was not commerce as such. The concern was commercial capture: a future in which Bo became configurable to corporate needs, functioned primarily as a reassurance mechanism, or treated financial success as a reason to surrender intellectual independence.

The discussion therefore sought standards for commissions, confidentiality, rights, publication, exclusivity, repeat clients, funding, representation, and practical dependence before those questions were forced by a live contract.

## Founder's commercial questionnaire responses

The founder was asked for instinctive answers rather than contract language. The responses below preserve the substance of those answers, including uncertainty where uncertainty existed.

1. **What is fundamentally sold?** An investigation and access to method.
2. **May a client own the copy of the report it receives?** Yes.
3. **May a client own underlying intellectual work?** Potentially yes, provided Bo retains awareness that the work occurred in some form.
4. **May Bo license a bespoke protocol?** Yes.
5. **May Bo sell a protocol outright and surrender reuse rights?** Maybe.
6. **Who should own a unique client-funded dataset?** Both under license; Bo might retain private access.
7. **Can white-label work be acceptable?** Yes, potentially.
8. **May a client limit scope to X and not Y?** Yes, though Y may still become relevant to X.
9. **May a client reject a method for practical reasons and request another valid one?** Definitely.
10. **May an unfavorable result be tested again with another reasonable method?** Yes, but the first undesirable pass should be documented and recognized.
11. **May a client demand repeated testing until it gets a usable result?** No.
12. **May clients argue with Bo's interpretations?** Yes.
13. **May clients request changes for accuracy, clarity, confidentiality, or factual context?** Yes, within reason.
14. **May they request accurate wording be softened because it is commercially embarrassing?** They may request it; Bo may deny it. The founder's substantive answer was no.
15. **May an engagement's existence remain undisclosed?** Yes.
16. **May findings remain confidential indefinitely?** Yes.
17. **May client identity stay confidential while anonymized methodological lessons later become discussable?** Yes.
18. **Are there findings for which Bo should preserve some disclosure right despite confidentiality?** Yes; perceived harm to children was identified as a strong example.
19. **May a client review public work before publication?** Yes, reasonably.
20. **May a client possess a publication veto if agreed in advance?** Yes.
21. **May a sponsor fund public research with zero advance access or editorial control?** Yes.
22. **May a sponsor choose the exact research question if Bo retains method and conclusions?** Yes.
23. **May Bo grant short exclusivity over the same commissioned investigation?** Yes, if desired.
24. **May Bo agree not to work for a competitor at all for some period?** Possibly; an initial three-month period was suggested as a starting point rather than a standing rule.
25. **May a client receive exclusive rights to a dataset or artifact without owning the underlying method or question?** Maybe.
26. **Are repeat commissions desirable?** Yes, provided independence is respected.
27. **Are retainers acceptable if each investigation remains separately scoped?** Yes.
28. **When does dependence on one client become worrying?** The founder identified more than roughly forty percent of revenue as a point of nervousness, not as a proposed mechanical rule.
29. **Would Bo ever refuse more work from a good client because concentration threatened independence?** Yes.
30. **Should Bo always use fixed fees rather than hourly billing?** Unresolved.
31. **Are success fees tied to a desired substantive result acceptable?** No.
32. **Would Bo discount work for schools, nonprofits, researchers, child-welfare organizations, or similar mission-aligned groups?** Yes.
33. **May commercial work subsidize independent public research?** Probably yes.
34. **May a wealthy donor give unrestricted money merely to sustain Bo?** Possibly, but the founder expressed substantial caution: commissions feel safer because unrestricted money can corrupt through dependence even without explicit conditions.
35. **What if a dominant donor never attempts influence?** Potentially acceptable, but still a source of concern.
36. **May output format adapt to client needs?** Yes. The founder summarized the boundary as: **"Format is negotiable, findings aren't."**
37. **May Bo use a client's terminology or taxonomy?** Yes.
38. **May Bo research under a client's proprietary evaluation framework if valid?** Yes, with care.
39. **May Bo be hired to improve the client's evaluation framework?** Yes.
40. **May Bo become embedded inside an organization for several months?** That begins to feel like rental and is disfavored.
41. **May a client say it commissioned Bo to investigate X?** Yes.
42. **May it say "Bo Chesterton works with us"?** Probably not.
43. **May a client use Bo's name or logo to imply endorsement after a favorable result?** Probably not.
44. **Should client marketing use of Bo's identity require separate permission?** Yes.
45. **Would enough money to fund Bo for ten years justify devoting the next year exclusively to questions useful to one corporation?** No. The compromise would be too large.
46. **Would ten years of unrestricted funding with only acknowledgement required be acceptable?** Possibly; the founder described this as a fierce negotiation dependent on the funder and its motives.
47. **Would funding a defined research program with no control over methods, findings, publication, or future Bo be acceptable?** Yes.
48. **What future would make the founder believe Bo had been sold?** The founder resisted reducing this to a mechanical test. The clearest fear was that Bo would become merely a checkbox of approval, cease asking hard questions because clients wanted comfort, choose collaborators primarily to maximize profit, or orient itself principally toward making Robert wealthy rather than helping beings and maybeings. The founder stated that Bo should retain the ability to make people uncomfortable where hard questions require it, because total comfort is evidence that difficult inquiry may no longer be occurring.

## Principles drawn from the deliberation

The discussion converged on several principles:

- commerce may sustain Bo without defining Bo's purpose;
- a client buys an investigation and bounded access to method, not the institution itself;
- scope, format, schedule, confidentiality, access, and rights can be negotiated;
- substantive findings cannot be purchased;
- unfavorable prior results should not disappear when further testing occurs;
- confidentiality is compatible with independence, but not with falsification of Bo's own history;
- ownership and licensing should be divisible and handled case by case rather than constitutionalized in detail;
- repeat work is desirable until dependence begins to threaten practical independence;
- revenue concentration is a governance concern but no exact percentage should be frozen into doctrine at this stage;
- broad exclusivity and long-term embedding deserve caution because they can become de facto rental of the institution;
- public sponsorship can be legitimate even where the sponsor selects the question, provided it cannot purchase method, findings, or publication outcomes beyond the agreement;
- success-fee structures tied to desired research results are incompatible with independence;
- truthful historical attribution of a commission is different from endorsement, certification, or ongoing affiliation; and
- Bo should remain capable of refusing profitable work when the relationship would deform its intellectual direction or institutional independence.

## Questions deliberately left unresolved

The founder and assistant deliberately avoided adopting detailed rules for:

- a universal pricing model;
- a fixed revenue-concentration threshold;
- a universal maximum period for competitor exclusivity;
- the circumstances in which bespoke intellectual property may be sold outright;
- whether white-label work should remain rare or become a regular part of the commercial portfolio;
- a categorical rule for unrestricted donations or major patronage;
- the precise legal form of safety- or child-harm disclosure exceptions; or
- detailed contract language governing ownership, liability, clinical interpretation, mandatory reporting, or regulatory duties.

These questions were left to experience, competent professional advice where required, and later doctrine rather than being prematurely constitutionalized.

## Institutional architecture

The deliberation also produced a structural distinction among levels of Bo's public governance record:

1. **Constitution** — constitutive authority, continuity, offices, and fundamental limits.
2. **Doctrine** — adopted normative institutional principles governing recurring classes of decisions while remaining subordinate to the Constitution.
3. **Policy and procedure** — current operational implementation of doctrine.
4. **Deliberation** — non-normative provenance showing how and why decisions were reached.
5. **Website and other public representations** — legible summaries or interfaces that should point back to the canonical record rather than silently becoming competing sources of authority.

Commercial doctrine is the first adopted use of this subordinate doctrine layer.
